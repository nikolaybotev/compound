# Part E. Paying early, getting out, and PMI

<a id="part-e"></a>

<a id="prepayment"></a>

## E1. Prepayment and prepayment penalties

**Prepayment** means paying principal before it is due: in full (sale or refinance) or in part (extra principal).

**Prepayment penalty** means "a charge imposed for paying all or part of the transaction's principal before the date on which the principal is due", with a narrow exception for waived bona fide third-party closing costs recouped on payoff within 36 months (**Verified**, [12 CFR 1026.32(b)(6)(i)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/chapter-X/subchapter-B/part-1026/subpart-E/section-1026.32)). Charging a full month's interest when the loan is paid off mid-month counts as a penalty (**Verified**, comment 32(b)(6)-1.i). **Inference:** from that comment, on a loan where penalties are banned, payoff interest runs only to the payoff date.

**When a penalty is even allowed** (**Verified**, [12 CFR 1026.43(g)(1)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/chapter-X/subchapter-B/part-1026/subpart-E/section-1026.43#p-1026.43(g))). All three conditions must hold:

1. the APR **cannot increase** after consummation (so **no ARM can have one**);
2. the loan is a **qualified mortgage**; and
3. it is **not a higher-priced mortgage loan**.

Even then, it is capped at **2%** of the amount prepaid in years 1–2 and **1%** in year 3, and nothing after year 3 (**Verified**, 12 CFR 1026.43(g)(2)). The creditor must also offer an alternative without a penalty (12 CFR 1026.43(g)(3)). State law can be stricter.

**Where to check:** Loan Estimate and Closing Disclosure page 1, "Prepayment Penalty: YES/NO", and the note's "Borrower's Right to Prepay" section.

<a id="extra-principal"></a>

## E2. Partial prepayments and extra principal

What the uniform ARM note says (**Verified:** Fannie Mae/Freddie Mac uniform ARM note, "Borrower's Right to Prepay"):

- "I have the right to make payments of Principal at any time before they are due. A payment of Principal only is known as a 'Prepayment.' When I make a Prepayment, I will notify the Note Holder in writing that I am doing so."
- "I may not designate a payment as a Prepayment if I have not made all the Monthly Payments then due."
- "The Note Holder will use my Prepayments to reduce the amount of Principal that I owe… However, the Note Holder may apply my Prepayment to the accrued and unpaid interest on the Prepayment amount" first.
- "If I make a partial Prepayment, there will be no changes in the due date of my Monthly Payment unless the Note Holder agrees in writing."
- **ARM-specific:** "My partial Prepayment may reduce the amount of my Monthly Payments after the first Change Date following my partial Prepayment. However, any reduction due to my partial Prepayment may be offset by an interest rate increase."

**What that means in practice.** On a fixed-rate loan, extra principal shortens the loan; the payment stays the same unless a recast is requested. On an ARM, the payment stays the same until the next change date. Then it is recalculated on the lower balance, so extra principal lowers the future payment. The concepts skill states that re-amortization and does not compute it here.

> **Illustration.** A 7/1 ($400,000, 6.25%). A one-time $20,000 extra principal payment in month 36 lowers the balance at the first change date from ≈ $360,134 to ≈ $334,471. If the rate then resets to 8.25%, the new payment is ≈ **$2,708** instead of ≈ $2,916. Not a reader's loan and not the page's $600,000 scenario.

**"Paid ahead" vs principal.** Money that is not clearly labeled as principal can be treated as an early **installment** instead. That advances the next due date ("paid ahead") and does not cut principal right away. Servicers must credit a full periodic payment as of the day it is received. How extra amounts are applied follows the loan contract (**Verified**, [12 CFR 1026.36(c)(1)(i)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/chapter-X/subchapter-B/part-1026/subpart-E/section-1026.36) and comment 36(c)(1)(i)-2). Practical steps (**Inference**, generic):

1. Be current first: make the regular payment in full.
2. Send the extra as a **separate** principal-only payment, or use the "additional principal" field. Do not use "next payment" or "future installment".
3. Put it in writing, as the note requires: a memo line "apply to principal only, not to future installments", or the online option that says the same.
4. Check the next periodic statement. Principal should drop by the full extra amount, and the next due date should **not** move.
5. Ask whether there is a minimum amount for a principal-only payment, and whether a recast is available on a fixed-rate loan.

**Do not confuse this with "Partial Payments" on the Closing Disclosure.** That Closing Disclosure section is about payments *smaller* than the amount due: whether they are accepted, held in a suspense account, or rejected (**Verified**, 12 CFR 1026.38(l)(5); 12 CFR 1026.36(c)(1)(ii)).

<a id="conversion"></a>

## E3. Conversion option vs refinancing

- **Conversion option:** some ARM notes let the borrower convert to a fixed rate at set times. CHARM (**Verified**, "Conversion option"): "When you convert, the new rate is generally set using a formula given in your loan documents. That fixed rate may be higher or lower than interest rates available to you in the market at that time. Also your lender may charge you a conversion fee." Fannie Mae has had convertible note forms (for example Form 3512, "Fixed Rate Conversion Option"). If a note has a conversion option, it is a variable-rate feature that must be disclosed (**Verified**, official commentary to §1026.18(f), "Conversion feature").
- **Refinancing:** a **new loan** that pays off the old one, with a new application, underwriting, appraisal, closing costs, Loan Estimate, and Closing Disclosure. Regulation Z: "A refinancing is a new transaction requiring new disclosures" (**Verified**, [12 CFR 1026.20(a)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/chapter-X/subchapter-B/part-1026/subpart-C/section-1026.20)).
- **Trade-off (Inference):** a conversion is usually cheaper and does not require re-qualifying, but its rate comes from the note's formula (often a market index plus a spread), which may not be the best rate available. A refinance can find a market rate but costs more, and requires qualifying again, including on income and on home value. CHARM's caution (**Verified**, "Is an adjustable-rate mortgage right for you?"): "Don't count on being able to refinance… You might not qualify for refinancing if the value of your home goes down."

<a id="assumable"></a>

## E4. What does "assumable" mean?

An assumable loan can be taken over by a buyer of the home **on its existing terms**, with approval. The Loan Estimate and Closing Disclosure must say whether "a subsequent purchaser of the property may be permitted to assume the remaining loan obligation on its original terms" (**Verified**, 12 CFR 1026.37(m)(2); 12 CFR 1026.38(l)(1)).

- Most conventional fixed-rate loans have an enforceable **due-on-sale** clause and are not assumable.
- Fannie Mae lets servicers approve a transfer to a **creditworthy purchaser** for most ARMs. The exceptions include certain plans, ARMs with what that guide calls "extended fixed-rate periods" (a long **initial period**) if the transfer happens during that period, and ARMs already converted to a fixed rate. Mortgage insurer approval is also required where applicable (**Verified**, [Servicing Guide D1-4.2-02](https://servicing-guide.fanniemae.com/svc/d1-4.2-02/conventional-mortgage-loans-include-due-sale-or-due-transfer-provision)).
- A portfolio ARM follows its own note and rider. Read the "Transfer of the Property" clause.

<a id="seasoning"></a>

## E5. What is "seasoning"?

**Seasoning** is how long a loan has existed and been paid, usually counted from closing or from the first payment. Several rules key off it:

- **PMI removal based on current value** (Fannie Mae): 2–5 years or more than 5 years (see [E6](#pmi-removal)).
- **Seasoned qualified mortgage** (Regulation Z): a fixed-rate, fully amortizing first lien held in portfolio can become a qualified-mortgage safe harbor after a **36-month seasoning period** starting at the first payment due date, if it has at most two 30-day delinquencies and no 60-day delinquencies (**Verified**, [12 CFR 1026.43(e)(7)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/chapter-X/subchapter-B/part-1026/subpart-E/section-1026.43#p-1026.43(e)(7))). ARMs are not eligible.
- **Refinance and other investor rules:** cash-out refinances and some products have their own seasoning periods. They vary by investor, so check the specific program (**Inference**; not itemized here).

<a id="pmi-removal"></a>

## E6. Removing PMI: the federal law vs Fannie Mae's current-value route

**Federal law: Homeowners Protection Act** (**Verified**, [12 U.S.C. 4901](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/12/4901) and [4902](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/12/4902)). It is based on **original value**: the lesser of the contract sales price and the appraised value at closing.

- **Borrower-requested cancellation at 80%:** the balance reaches 80% of original value, either on the amortization schedule or **by actual payments** (so extra principal gets there sooner). Conditions: a written request, a good payment history, being current, and, if the holder requires it, evidence that the value has not declined below original value and that there is no subordinate lien.
- **Automatic termination at 78%:** the date the balance is *scheduled* to reach 78% of original value, if the loan is current. Extra payments do not move this date. For an ARM, the schedule "then in effect" is used.
- **Final termination at the midpoint** of the amortization period (year 15 of a 30-year loan), if the loan is current.
- Unearned premiums must be returned within 45 days.
- Exception: some "high risk" loans follow investor or holder rules instead.

**Fannie Mae: cancellation based on current value** (**Verified**, [Servicing Guide B-8.1-04](https://servicing-guide.fanniemae.com/svc/b-8.1-04/termination-conventional-mortgage-insurance)), for a one-unit principal residence or second home, at the borrower's request:

- **Seasoning 2–5 years:** LTV of **75%** or less of the *current* value.
- **Seasoning more than 5 years:** LTV of **80%** or less of current value.
- **Improvements that add value** can waive the two-year minimum. In that case LTV must be 80% or less.
- The value must come from a valuation based on an interior and exterior inspection, ordered through Fannie Mae's system. The payment record must be clean: current, no 30-day late payments in 12 months, and no 60-day late payments in 24 months.
- **Verified:** [Fannie Mae Lender Letter LL-2026-07](https://singlefamily.fanniemae.com/media/48106/display), September 16, 2026, now lets servicers proactively solicit borrowers who may qualify based on current value. Until then, servicers could only respond to requests.
- These are Fannie Mae's rules. Freddie Mac, FHA, and portfolio loans have their own.

> **Illustration.** Purchase price $500,000, appraisal $505,000, so original value is $500,000. Loan $450,000 (90% LTV).
>
> - Homeowners Protection Act request point: balance $400,000. Automatic termination: balance scheduled to hit $390,000.
> - Fannie Mae current-value route in year 3: a new valuation of $580,000 and a balance of $432,000 gives 74.5%, which is at or under 75%, so it qualifies. In year 6 the test becomes 80% or under.
>
> Not a reader's home or loan.
